Every brand asks the same first question, and most factory websites answer it with a number that turns out to be fiction: *what is your MOQ?*

We do not publish one. Not because we are being cagey, but because a single figure would be wrong for roughly half the styles we are asked to quote. Here is what actually sets it.

## The three things that set a minimum

### 1. The fabric mill's minimum, not ours

This is usually the binding constraint, and it has nothing to do with the garment factory. A mill weaving a custom quality will have its own minimum per colour — commonly in the region of a few thousand metres. If your style needs 1.8 metres per piece, that mill minimum has already decided your MOQ before a single machine is loaded.

Stock or open-market fabric changes the picture completely. If the base is something we or our suppliers already hold, the fabric minimum largely disappears and the constraint moves to the next item on this list.

**The practical consequence:** the fastest way to lower a minimum is to accept an existing fabric quality rather than developing one.

### 2. Colour count

MOQ is almost always *per colour*, not per style. A 2,000-piece order in one colourway is a straightforward run. The same 2,000 pieces split across five colourways is five separate fabric lots, five dye batches, five print or embroidery setups and five line changeovers.

Brands routinely underestimate this. Splitting a style across colours is the single most common reason a quote comes back higher than expected.

### 3. Decoration setup

Setup cost is fixed and has to be amortised across the run. Digitising an embroidery design, cutting screens for a print, matching a colour standard through strike-offs — that work costs the same whether you order 300 pieces or 3,000.

This is where in-house capability changes the economics. Because we own our embroidery machines, we are not paying a subcontractor's setup margin on top of our own, so the minimum at which an embroidered style becomes viable is lower than it would be if we were buying that process in. Printing we run through partner houses, and there the print table's own minimum applies.

## What "lead time" actually contains

Lead time quoted by a factory almost never means what a brand assumes it means. Break it into its real components:

| Stage | What is happening | Who controls it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Development | Pattern, proto, fit rounds, strike-offs | Shared — your comment turnaround matters as much as ours |
| Fabric | Mill production or sourcing, dyeing, in-house inspection | The mill |
| Production | Cutting, sewing, decoration, finishing | The factory |
| Inspection | Final AQL, third-party if appointed | Shared |
| Freight | EXW to your door | Your forwarder |

A factory quoting "30 days" is quoting the production window only. That same order is commonly 45 to 60 days to your warehouse once fabric and freight are counted. Neither figure is dishonest; they answer different questions. Ask which one you are being given.

The stage brands most often underestimate is their own: **fit comment turnaround**. A development that could close in three weeks routinely takes six because comments sat in an inbox. If your calendar is tight, the highest-leverage thing you control is answering sample comments the week they arrive.

## How to get a real number quickly

Send these four things together and you will get a costing rather than a chain of follow-up questions:

- The garment specification — a tech pack, a sketch, or a physical reference sample
- Estimated quantity **per style and per colour**
- The fabric quality you have in mind, or a swatch
- Your target delivery window

With those, a factory can tell you the minimum that actually applies to your style, and whether the delivery date is real. Without them, any MOQ you are quoted is a guess dressed up as a policy.

## The question worth asking instead

Rather than "what is your MOQ", ask: *at what quantity does this style stop being expensive per unit?*

That question gets you the useful answer — the point where setup is properly amortised and the price curve flattens. It is a different number for every style, and any factory that has costed your specification can tell you where it sits.
