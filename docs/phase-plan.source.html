How the website is built — in plain language

Written so the technical choices can be understood, questioned and defended without a developer in the room.

Next.js and React — the foundation

The site is built on Next.js (version 16), which is the current professional standard for building business websites, and React, the technology underneath it. Netflix, Nike, TikTok, Walmart and OpenAI all run public websites on this same foundation. It is free and open-source: there is no licence to renew and no vendor who can raise a price on you.

Why this matters commercially, not just technically

  • The pages are pre-built. Each page is generated in advance rather than assembled while the visitor waits, so a buyer in Europe opens it in well under a second. Google treats speed as a ranking factor.
  • The text is in the page itself. Many modern sites deliver an empty page and fill it in afterwards. Search engines tolerate this; AI crawlers frequently do not. Here every word is in the raw page.
  • It scales without a rewrite. The admin panel, a buyer login or a second language are additions to this foundation, not reasons to start again.
  • It is not locked to us. The code is standard, documented and owned by Saroz Threadz. Any competent agency could take it over — you should keep us because the work is good, not because leaving is expensive.

Where it will live

The site is designed to be hosted on Vercel, the company that builds Next.js. Copies are served from data centres worldwide, so it loads quickly in every market you sell to; certificates, backups and scaling are automatic. Every change is published to a private preview link for approval before it reaches the public site.

Phase 1 — delivered

The public website, complete and tested. Below is what exists today, including the four items completed in this most recent round of work.

Completed in this round

1. A display fault that affected every visitor — fixed

Pages reached by clicking a link in the menu could appear blank, and only showed their content if the visitor reloaded. The cause: the site fades content in gently as you scroll, and the code responsible was only being told about the first page a visitor opened. On every page after that, the content was present but left invisible.

It is now corrected and verified in a real browser: navigating between pages by tapping links on a phone, no element stays hidden, and no errors are raised. This was the single most damaging defect on the site — a buyer who sees a blank page does not reload, they leave.

2. Products now shown as cards

The products page previously required scrolling through five long sections to learn what the factory makes. It now opens with a card for every category — photograph, name, a one-line description and the fabrics — so the full range is visible at a glance. On a phone the cards sit two to a row, which was a deliberate decision: one card per screen would have meant five screens of scrolling to answer a question that should take three seconds.

3. Admin panel preview

A new page shows exactly what the Phase 2 admin panel will look like: the add-product form, the photograph upload, and every product listed as a card with Edit and Remove. Every control is deliberately switched off. It is the real layout, not a drawing, so the screen can be reviewed and changed now — while changes are cheap — rather than after it is built. The page is closed to search engines and AI crawlers.

4. TechLeaf identity and group links

A single quiet line closes every page: a small green leaf mark and Designed & built by TechLeaf, on black, below the legal row. Green appears nowhere else on the site, so it reads as a maker's mark on finished work rather than an advertisement inside a client's website. Beside it, a small group row links to Asia by Locals, Guide India Tours and Akurock.

A professional note on those links. We placed them as small, quiet, but genuinely visible links rather than hiding them in invisible text. Hidden links are classed as a link scheme by Google: they pass no value to the destination sites and they put the whole domain at risk of a penalty. A discreet, labelled row in the footer is unobtrusive to a reader and actually passes authority to the three sites — which is the entire reason for having them.

04

AI search: SEO, AEO and GEO

A sourcing manager looking for a manufacturer in 2026 does not begin with ten blue links. They ask an assistant, get three or four names, and visit those websites. If the assistant does not know Saroz Threadz, the website is never opened — however good it is.

The three terms, properly defined

SEO — Search Engine Optimisation
Being found in the classic list of Google results. Still the largest single source of enquiries, and still worth winning.
AEO — Answer Engine Optimisation
Being the source behind a direct answer. When someone asks "what is the minimum order quantity at a Jaipur dress factory?", one site supplies that answer. Pages must be built so a single passage fully answers a single question.
GEO — Generative Engine Optimisation
Being named and cited inside an answer written by ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity or Google's AI Overviews. This is the newest of the three and the one most competitors have not begun. Being early is the advantage.

What the site already does for this

  • One page per question a buyer actually asks. A single products page cannot be the answer to six different questions. Six pages can. Each product and capability page is built around a real buyer question and answers it in the first paragraph.
  • Structured data. Every page carries a hidden, machine-readable description of the company, the factory, the products and the FAQ, in the Schema.org format Google and the AI crawlers read. This is how a machine learns "manufacturer, Jaipur, womenswear, SMETA audited" as fact rather than guesswork.
  • An llms.txt file. An emerging standard: a clean summary of the site written for AI systems rather than people. Few Indian manufacturers have one.
  • AI crawlers named and allowed explicitly. The instructions file names OpenAI's, Anthropic's, Perplexity's, Google's and Microsoft's crawlers and grants them access, removing any ambiguity. The admin panel is excluded.
  • Answers in the text, not in images. Numbers, certifications and processes are written as text a machine can quote. A figure inside a photograph is invisible to every crawler.
  • An Insights section. First-hand explanation from the factory floor is the single strongest driver of AI citation, because assistants prefer sources with original knowledge over sources that repeat others.
The honest constraint. AI systems weigh credibility heavily, and credibility comes from specifics: real numbers, named clients, real photographs of the floor. The site is engineered correctly, but a page reading "minimum order quantity: on request" cannot be cited by an assistant answering a question about minimum order quantity. Section 07 is therefore not administrative housekeeping — it is the highest-value work remaining, and only Saroz Threadz can do it.
05

Design and usability decisions

Recorded here so they can be reviewed and reversed on request. Every point below is a decision, not a default.

The phone comes first

More than half of first visits to a manufacturer's website are on a phone, often a buyer checking a supplier between meetings. Every screen was designed at phone width and then expanded, rather than the usual reverse. Specifically: buttons and links are at least 44 pixels tall so they can be tapped accurately; nothing scrolls sideways at any width; the product cards sit two to a row so the range is visible without scrolling; the menu opens as a full-height panel rather than a cramped dropdown; and text never falls below a comfortable reading size.

A restrained palette, used with intent

Near-black, greys and white, with a single orange used as a signal — one marked item per screen, so attention lands where it should. Nothing is rounded and structure is drawn with fine rules rather than shadows. The intent is an industrial, engineered impression: this is a factory, not a fashion label.

Honesty as a design principle

Where a figure is not confirmed, the site says so rather than inventing one. Where the factory does not make a category — knitwear, denim, outerwear — the products page states it plainly. Buyers screen suppliers, and a supplier who says "we do not do that" is more credible than one who claims everything.

Accessible by construction

Text contrast meets the WCAG AA standard, every image carries a description, the whole site can be operated by keyboard, and animation is switched off automatically for visitors whose devices request reduced motion. Several of your buyers are large retailers with accessibility requirements in their supplier terms; this is checked during their onboarding.

Open review points

The following are ready to change on your instruction, and are quick to change now:

  • The signal colour. Orange is the current choice; it can become a deeper red, a brass or a green if the family prefers.
  • Card density on phones — two per row as built, or one larger card if you would rather lead with the photography.
  • Whether the product cards should also replace the plain category row on the home page.
  • The wording of the headline on the home page and the closing call to action.
