Ask a supplier whether they do embroidery in house. The answer tells you more about your delivery date than almost any other question you can ask.

## The mechanism, not the marketing

When embroidery is subcontracted, here is what physically happens to your order. Cut panels leave the garment factory before assembly. They travel to a second company. They join that company's queue, behind whatever else it is running — including work for factories that give it more volume than yours does. They are stitched. They travel back. Only then can your garment be assembled.

Two things go wrong in that loop, and they are different problems.

**Schedule risk.** Your panels are now sitting in a queue that your factory does not control and cannot see. A supplier can be entirely honest about their own capacity and still miss your date because a third party reprioritised. This is the single most common cause of late shipment on decorated womenswear, and it is invisible from the outside until it happens.

**Consistency drift.** The sample was stitched carefully, probably by the vendor's best operator, on a machine set up for a one-off. Bulk is stitched at production speed, possibly on a different machine, possibly at a different tension. Density changes. Puckering appears on fabric that behaved perfectly at sample stage. The garment is not *wrong*, exactly — it is just not what you approved.

## What ownership actually fixes

We own our embroidery machines and run them on our own floor in Jaipur. The difference is not that we are better at embroidery than a specialist vendor — some vendors are excellent. The difference is structural:

- **Embroidery is scheduled against our own line plan.** Panels do not leave the building, so decorated styles can run on the same lead time as plain ones instead of carrying a subcontractor's queue as dead time.
- **Strike-offs are stitched on the bulk fabric.** Density and pucker are judged on the base the garment will actually be made from, not on a substitute the vendor had to hand.
- **Fit and embroidery are revised together.** When a fit comment moves a yoke seam, the embroidery placement moves with it in the same round. Split across two companies, that is two rounds and two weeks.
- **One company carries the risk.** If the embroidery is wrong, there is no conversation about whose fault it is. It is ours.

## Where we do not own the process

Printing. We say so plainly, on the printing page and here.

Print runs through partner houses in the Jaipur, Sanganer and Bagru belt — a region that has been printing textiles for centuries — and we have used the same ones for decades. We manage the strike-offs, the colour approval and the quality control, and we carry the risk with the buyer. But the tables are not ours, and a factory that pretends otherwise is telling you something about how it handles the rest of the truth.

The honest position is not "we do everything in house". Almost no one does. The honest position is a clear line between what is ours and what is a partner's, so a buyer can price the risk accurately.

## Four questions worth asking any supplier

1. **Is embroidery done on your own machines, on this site?** If not, how far away is the vendor and how many other factories do they serve?
2. **Will the strike-off be stitched on the bulk fabric?** A strike-off on a substitute base tells you very little about pucker.
3. **Who re-digitises when a fit comment moves the placement?** If the answer involves a third party, add a week per round.
4. **If bulk embroidery does not match the approved sample, who pays?** The answer reveals where the risk actually sits.

None of these require a factory visit, and the answers are hard to fudge.
